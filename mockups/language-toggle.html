Language control: seven options

Every masthead below is the real one from index.html, same tokens, same spacing, at 360px phone width. Tap any control to flip it and see both states. Option A is what is live today.

Theme Page language
A Two letters, target language Live today

One pill showing the language you would switch to. Smallest possible footprint. The catch: a pill reading “SW” is equally readable as “you are in Swahili”, and there is nothing else on screen to disambiguate until the page is already translated.

Nairobi Open 2026
Trade-off: smallest and quietest, but ambiguous about which state you are in.
B Segmented, two letters Recommended

Both languages always visible, the active one filled in accent. Removes A's ambiguity for about 26px more width, and a segmented control is a pattern people already know from every settings screen.

Nairobi Open 2026
Trade-off: unambiguous and still compact. Two tap targets instead of one, each a little smaller.
C Segmented, flags only

Kenyan flag for Swahili, American flag for English. Reads instantly across a language barrier and needs no reading at all, which is its whole appeal.

Nairobi Open 2026
Worth thinking about before you pick this. English is an official language of Kenya, so a Kenyan flag standing for “Swahili” and an American flag standing for “English” tells a Kenyan player that the Kenyan option is the other one. A British flag has the same problem with extra history attached. Flags also render differently on every phone: Android shows them, older Windows browsers show “KE” and “US” letter boxes instead.
D Segmented, flag plus code

The flag carries recognition, the two letters carry the meaning, so it still works on a phone that cannot draw the emoji. Widest of the options.

Nairobi Open
Trade-off: most explicit, but at ~112px it starts squeezing the wordmark on a 360px screen. Shown here with the shorter “Nairobi Open” for that reason.
E One pill, the language's own name Also good

Each language written the way its own speakers write it: in English mode the pill offers Kiswahili, in Swahili mode it offers English. Self-explanatory with no legend, no flag, and no politics, and it is the convention most international sites landed on.

Nairobi Open 2026
Trade-off: clearest single-button option. Still technically ambiguous the same way A is, but a word people recognise as “the other language” makes it read as an offer rather than a status.
F Globe plus current code

The globe is the near-universal “language” icon, so the pill announces what it is before anyone reads the letters. Here the letters mean the language you are in, which is the opposite of A and the reason the globe is doing real work.

Nairobi Open 2026
Trade-off: best affordance of the one-button options; the emoji globe renders as flat line art on some Androids and can look slightly off next to the club badge.
G Segmented, full words

Nothing to decode at all. It is the widest control here, so the wordmark has to give up room; on a 360px phone “Nairobi Open 2026” will not fit alongside it.

Nairobi Open
Trade-off: zero ambiguity, but it competes with the wordmark for the top line instead of sitting quietly beside it.

One small bug spotted while building this: the live button's aria-label is hardcoded to “Badilisha lugha”, so a screen reader announces it in Swahili even in English mode. Whichever option you pick, that label should follow the language.
